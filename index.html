Myth vs. fact

Common misconceptions, covered.

Myth

Are all snakes slimy?

Most snakes feel dry and smooth.

Fact

Reality

Snake skin is usually dry, scaled, and sleek.

The “slimy” idea comes from wet or muddy conditions or from confusion with amphibians like frogs and salamanders.

Myth

Do all snakes bite?

Most snakes avoid confrontation.

Fact

Reality

Most species prefer to flee or hide.

Bites are usually defensive and happen when a snake feels trapped, startled, or threatened.

Myth

Poisonous vs. venomous?

They are not the same thing.

Fact

Reality

Venomous animals inject toxins; poisonous ones are toxic to eat or touch.

Snakes are typically described as venomous because they inject venom through specialized glands and fangs.

Anatomy

Built for survival: key adaptations.

Heat-sensing pits

Infrared vision helps predators track warm-blooded prey.

Pit vipers and some pythons use heat-sensitive organs to detect temperature differences in the environment. This helps them locate prey even in low light and detect whether a target is nearby.

  • Helps locate warm mammals like mice and birds at night.
  • Improves hunting precision in forests, caves, and near water.

Flexible jaw

A modular skull allows huge meals and powerful swallowing.

Snake jaws are not fused together like ours. Many bones are linked by flexible ligaments, allowing the mouth to open wide and stretch around prey much larger than the head itself.

  • Can consume prey larger than their own head circumference.
  • This adaptation helps them survive on infrequent, high-energy meals.

Camouflage

Pattern and color help snakes vanish into their surroundings.

Many snake species rely on mottled scales, stripes, and earth-tone coloration to disappear in leaves, bark, sand, or grass. Camouflage helps both hunting and avoiding larger predators.

  • Increases hunting success by making ambush less obvious.
  • Protects them from hawks, big cats, and other rainforest threats.

Rattle mechanism

A warning system designed to reduce dangerous encounters.

Rattlesnakes vibrate specialized keratin segments at the tail tip to create a warning sound. It is often a warning before striking and is most effective when the snake feels threatened before contact.

  • Serves as a non-lethal warning to large mammals and humans.
  • Best understood as a behavior that reduces conflict, not a sign of aggression.

Safety guide

What to do when you encounter a snake.

Do

  • Stay calm and give the snake space to move away.
  • Keep children and pets at a safe distance.
  • Watch where you step and wear secure footwear outdoors.
  • If bitten, seek emergency medical care immediately and try to remember the snake’s appearance from a safe distance.

Don't

  • Do not try to handle, trap, or provoke the snake.
  • Do not cut the wound or suck out venom.
  • Do not apply a tourniquet or tight band around the bitten limb.
  • Do not wait for symptoms to worsen—prompt medical attention matters most.
